## A. Information theory of active learning: what do "transductive"/pool criteria actually target?

The trivial identity in the prompt — for x drawn from the same finite pool R whose labels Y_R are the object of interest, I(Y_x; Y_R | D) collapses to H(Y_x | D) when x ∈ R and Y_x is a coherent (deterministic, already-in-R) component of Y_R — is indeed trivial and is *not* what the cited "information gain" criteria compute. The literature's headline criteria target one of three different random variables, and the distinctions are explicit and load-bearing in the papers themselves:

**1. BALD targets the *parameters*, not the pool.**
Houlsby, Huszár, Ghahramani, Lengyel, "Bayesian Active Learning for Classification and Preference Learning," arXiv:1112.5745 (2011) — https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.5745, PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/1112.5745. BALD's score (their Eq. 2) is
  argmax_x  H[y | x, D] − E_{θ~p(θ|D)} H[y | x, θ],
which the paper identifies as the mutual information I(θ, y | x, D) between the candidate label y and the model parameters θ, *not* between y and any other pool label. The paper's own framing: they "seek the x for which the model is marginally most uncertain about y ... but for which individual settings of the parameters are confident" — i.e., disagreement among parameter-conditional predictors, not information about the remaining pool's labels. This is the "model-based"/parameter-space branch of active-learning information criteria, and every "pool-aware"/"test-distribution-aware" paper below defines itself in explicit contrast to this fact.

**2. "Transductive experimental design" targets predictive/reconstruction error on the *remaining unlabeled pool*, not the model.**
Yu, Bi, Tresp, "Active Learning via Transductive Experimental Design," ICML 2006 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1143844.1143980; PDF: https://www.dbs.ifi.lmu.de/~tresp/papers/icml2006_actv.pdf. Confirmed: authors Kai Yu, Jinbo Bi, Volker Tresp (Siemens), ICML 2006, Pittsburgh. Their objective (Definition 3.1) is
  max_{X⊂V, |X|=m}  Tr[ V Xᵀ(XXᵀ + μI)⁻¹ X Vᵀ ],
which explicitly selects the labeled subset X to minimize expected squared prediction error over the *full remaining unlabeled set V* (the pool), i.e., a reconstruction/regression criterion on the pool's outputs, not an information-theoretic statement about a hypothesis or parameter posterior. This is the design-theoretic (A/D/E-optimality-style) ancestor of "pool-based" active learning, distinct in kind from BALD.

**3. MacKay (1992) explicitly separates "total information" (about parameters) from other objectives, and is the origin of the information-based framing that BALD descends from.**
MacKay, "Information-Based Objective Functions for Active Data Selection," Neural Computation 4(4):590–604, 1992 — https://direct.mit.edu/neco/article/4/4/590/5648/Information-Based-Objective-Functions-for-Active; https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/efefp-2j353. (Full text not re-derived here beyond the confirmed title/venue/year and its well-documented role as originator of "expected information gain about model parameters" and, separately, predictive-variance/expected-squared-error objectives for active data selection — this is the paper that first distinguishes objectives targeting the *parameters* from objectives targeting *predictions*, the same fork that BALD vs. EPIG re-derives 30 years later.)

**4. Zhu, Lafferty, Ghahramani (2003): an explicit expected-risk-on-the-pool criterion.**
"Combining Active Learning and Semi-Supervised Learning Using Gaussian Fields and Harmonic Functions," ICML-2003 Workshop on The Continuum from Labeled to Unlabeled Data — PDF confirmed at https://mlg.eng.cam.ac.uk/zoubin/papers/zglactive.pdf. Confirmed exact criterion: x* = argmin_x R̂(f_x), i.e., they pick the query that minimizes the *expected estimated risk* on the (harmonic-function) classifier, computed by averaging the post-query risk over the two possible label outcomes weighted by current predicted probability. This is a direct forebear of Roy & McCallum's expected-error-reduction (below) specialized to graph-based semi-supervised learning, and it "thinks globally" (i.e., about the whole unlabeled pool's expected loss), not about parameter entropy.

**5. Kirsch, Rainforth, Gal — "Test Distribution-Aware Active Learning" (arXiv:2106.11719, 2021) — states the BALD-vs-pool distinction as its main thesis.**
https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11719, PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.11719. Confirmed abstract statement: "conventional model-based methods for active learning — like BALD — have a fundamental shortfall: they fail to directly account for the test-time distribution." Their proposed acquisition function is
  I[Y^eval ; Y^acq | X^eval, x^acq, D^train],
mutual information between the candidate label and predictions on a set of *evaluation/target* inputs X^eval — explicitly contrasted against BALD's parameter-space I[Y^acq; Ω | x^acq, D^train]. This paper is the direct ancestor of EPIG.

**6. Smith, Kirsch, Farquhar, Gal, Foster, Rainforth — EPIG (arXiv:2304.08151, AISTATS 2023, published as Bickford Smith et al., "Prediction-Oriented Bayesian Active Learning," PMLR v206).**
https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08151; PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.08151; PMLR https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/bickfordsmith23a.html. Confirmed abstract: "Information-theoretic approaches to active learning have traditionally focused on maximising the information gathered about the model parameters, most commonly by optimising the BALD score... We propose ... the expected predictive information gain (EPIG), an acquisition function that measures information gain in the space of predictions rather than parameters." Exact formula confirmed from the PDF:
  EPIG(x) = E_{p*(x*)} E_{p(y,y*|x,x*)} [ log( p(y,y*|x,x*) / (p(y|x) p(y*|x*)) ) ],
i.e., I(y ; y* | x, x*) averaged over a target input distribution p*(x*) — mutual information between the candidate label and the (unknown) label of a randomly drawn *target-distribution* point, explicitly NOT the pool per se but a designated "input of interest" distribution (which can be set to the pool or to a held-out test distribution). Confirmed contrast: "Whereas BALD targets information gain in model parameters, EPIG directly targets a reduction in predictive uncertainty on inputs of interest."

**7. Mussmann & Liang (2018): a Fisher-information / asymptotic-efficiency account of *uncertainty sampling*, not information about the pool.**
"On the Relationship between Data Efficiency and Error for Uncertainty Sampling," ICML 2018 (PMLR v80) — https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/mussmann18a.html; arXiv:1806.06123, PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.06123. Confirmed main result: they relate the asymptotic *data efficiency* DE(ε) of two-stage uncertainty sampling to the limiting error rate Err via DE(ε) ≈ 1/(4·Err), derived by comparing Fisher information matrices of active (boundary-concentrated) vs. passive (random) sampling — uncertainty sampling wins because it concentrates samples where σ=1/2, maximizing Fisher information orthogonal to the true parameter direction. This is an efficiency/statistical argument, not a claim that uncertainty sampling maximizes I(y_x; pool) or I(y_x; θ) per se — it is compatible with, but distinct from, the BALD framing.

**8. Freund, Seung, Shamir, Tishby (1997), Query-By-Committee.**
"Selective Sampling Using the Query by Committee Algorithm," Machine Learning 28:133–168, 1997 — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1007330508534. Confirmed core mechanism: a two-member (or larger) committee sampled from the posterior over hypotheses is used to filter a stream of unlabeled points; a point is queried when the committee disagrees. Confirmed abstract-level claim: "if the committee algorithm achieves information gain with positive lower bound, then the prediction error decreases exponentially with the number of queries" — i.e., QBC disagreement is used as a *proxy* for information gain about the hypothesis (version space), and the paper's generalization-error bounds are stated in terms of that information gain, which is a **parameter/hypothesis-space** quantity (disagreement = a proxy for the version-space entropy), not a pool-label quantity. QBC is thus in the "target the hypothesis/parameters" camp with BALD, of which it is a precursor (BALD is sometimes described as a Bayesian, single-model relaxation of QBC).

**9. Golovin & Krause (2011), adaptive submodularity — generalizes Dasgupta's greedy analysis.**
"Adaptive Submodularity: Theory and Applications in Active Learning and Stochastic Optimization," JAIR 42:427–486, 2011 — https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/10731; arXiv:1003.3967. This paper proves that (noiseless) generalized binary search / greedy version-space-halving active learning is a special case of adaptively maximizing an adaptive-submodular, adaptive-monotone objective (the "Generalized Binary Search" objective corresponds to a specific coverage function over hypotheses), giving a (1-1/e) or ln-approximation-style guarantee for greedy query selection that most reduces the surviving hypothesis set. This is the general theoretical umbrella under which Dasgupta's and Nowak's specific GBS results sit.

**10. Dasgupta (2005), "Analysis of a greedy active learning strategy" — the generalized-binary-search / version-space-halving result.**
NeurIPS/NIPS 2004 (published 2005), PDF confirmed at https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~dasgupta/papers/greedy.pdf. Confirmed content: the greedy strategy at each step queries the point that "most evenly divides the current effective version space" weighted by a prior π over the hypothesis class Ĥ, and the main theorem states that when π is uniform over Ĥ, "the expected number of labels needed by this greedy strategy is at most O(ln|Ĥ|) times that of any other strategy" (a competitive/near-optimality guarantee against the best possible query strategy, not literally an equivalence to entropy minimization stated as such in this paper's own language — the paper's own framing is in terms of "shrinkage"/probability mass eliminated from the version space, though this shrinkage criterion is exactly proportional to expected information gain about the (uniformly-a-priori) hypothesis when splits are binary).

**11. Nowak (2011), "The Geometry of Generalized Binary Search," IEEE Trans. Info. Theory 57(12):7893–7906 — makes the information-theoretic optimality connection explicit.**
https://arxiv.org/abs/0910.4397; PDF confirmed at https://nowak.ece.wisc.edu/GBS_arxiv_v3.pdf. Confirmed: "GBS is a well-known greedy algorithm for determining a binary-valued function through a sequence of strategically selected queries. At each step, a query is selected that most evenly splits the hypotheses under consideration into two disjoint subsets." Nowak defines a "coherence parameter" c* quantifying how close to a perfect 50/50 split the best available query can achieve at every step, and shows GBS achieves query complexity within a constant factor of the information-theoretic lower bound log₂N (N = number of hypotheses) whenever c* is bounded away from 1 — an explicit invocation of the Shannon-style "at least log₂N queries needed to identify one of N hypotheses" bound, tying the geometric bisection criterion directly to mutual-information-optimal search under a uniform prior.

**Bottom line on the specific claim in the prompt — "uncertainty sampling maximizes I(y_x; h) where h is the hypothesis/full labeling, for noiseless labels, i.e. generalized binary search = margin under a uniform prior on hypotheses":**
This is a known, standard statement in the active-learning theory literature, though it is usually phrased geometrically ("most-even split of the version space") rather than literally written as "argmax I(y_x; h)" in the primary sources fetched here. The equivalence is exact and well known: for a finite/discrete hypothesis class H with prior π and *noiseless, deterministic* labels (y_x = h(x) for the true h), querying the x that most evenly splits H into {h: h(x)=+1} and {h: h(x)=−1} is precisely the x that maximizes I(y_x; h) under prior π, because I(y_x;h) = H(y_x) − H(y_x|h) = H(y_x) (labels are deterministic given h, so the second term is 0), and the binary entropy H(y_x) is maximized exactly at a 50/50 split. Dasgupta (2005) and Golovin & Krause (2011) prove near-optimality guarantees for this greedy bisection strategy (in expected number of queries relative to the best possible strategy), and Nowak (2011) makes the connection to the information-theoretic query-complexity lower bound (log₂|H|) explicit and geometric. This family of results is the rigorous justification for treating "generalized binary search"/"most-uncertain point under a version-space measure" as literally information-optimal in the noiseless, finite-hypothesis-class setting — a genuinely different (and much more restrictive) setting than the general pool/BALD/EPIG discussion in items 1–7, which concern continuous parameter spaces, noisy labels, and Bayesian posteriors rather than a literal shrinking finite version space.

## B. Expected error reduction, decision flips, and "value of information is zero unless the decision changes"

**Roy & McCallum (2001).**
"Toward Optimal Active Learning through Sampling Estimation of Error Reduction," ICML 2001 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/645530.655646; Semantic Scholar record https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Toward-Optimal-Active-Learning-through-Sampling-of-Roy-McCallum/0a20a309deda54fe14580007759c9c7623c58694 (full abstract text could not be scraped directly from the mirrors tried, but the paper's well-documented method — confirmed across multiple secondary citations found in this search — is to estimate, for each unlabeled candidate x and each possible label y it might receive, the expected future generalization error of the retrained classifier, and to query the x that minimizes this expected future error, estimated by sampling/self-labeling over the pool). This is the classical **expected error reduction (EER)** framework, the direct ancestor of the MOCU/SMOCU line below and of Zhu-Lafferty-Ghahramani's R̂(f_x) criterion in item A.4.

**MOCU (Yoon, Dougherty, Qian, 2013) — the formal Bayes-risk statement of "value = 0 unless the decision/operator changes."**
Yoon, Dougherty, Qian, "Quantifying the Objective Cost of Uncertainty in Complex Dynamical Systems," IEEE Transactions on Signal Processing, vol. 61, no. 9, pp. 2394–2405, 2013 (IEEE Xplore record: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6473917; the PDF mirror at https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/iel7/78/6492148/06473917.pdf could not be fetched — 418 error from the proxy — but its definition is confirmed through the follow-on paper below, which restates it faithfully). Confirmed definition (from the follow-on generalized-MOCU paper, ar5iv https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1805.01143, which explicitly restates the original 2013 MOCU): MOCU "quantifies the performance cost of using an operator that is optimal *across* an uncertainty class of systems [i.e., the robust/Bayes-averaged action] as opposed to an operator that is optimal *for* [the true, particular] system." Formally, with a parameter/system θ ~ π(θ), the robust action ψ_π (optimal on average under π) and the θ-optimal action ψ_θ:
  MOCU(π) = E_θ[ C_θ(ψ_π) − C_θ(ψ_θ) ] ≥ 0,
with equality only when ψ_π = ψ_θ for (π-almost) every θ — i.e., the excess cost of "the one-size-fits-all robust decision" is exactly zero whenever the robust decision already coincides with the decision that would be optimal if θ were known. This *is* the formal expression of "value of information is zero unless the decision changes": an experiment that leaves the robust/MAP decision unchanged for every likely posterior realization contributes zero expected MOCU reduction.

**Zhao et al., SMOCU, AISTATS 2021 (note: not NeurIPS — corrected from the prompt).**
"Bayesian Active Learning by Soft Mean Objective Cost of Uncertainty," AISTATS 2021 — https://proceedings.mlr.press/v130/zhao21c.html; PDF http://proceedings.mlr.press/v130/zhao21c/zhao21c.pdf. Confirmed venue: **AISTATS 2021**, not NeurIPS as stated in the prompt — this should be corrected in any citation. Confirmed exact formulas fetched from the PDF:
  M(π(θ)) = E_x[ E_{π(θ)}[ C_θ(ψ_π(θ), x) − C_θ(ψ_θ, x) ] ]  (MOCU, adapted to per-query x)
  M_s(π(θ)) = E_x{ E_{π(θ)}[max_y p(y|x,θ)] − (1/k) log[ Σ_y exp(k·p(y|x)) ] }  (soft/differentiable relaxation, SMOCU).
Confirmed explicit zero-value statement, their Lemma 2: "∀x∈X, U_s(x;π(θ)) ≥ 0; the equality only holds for the case π(θ)=π(θ|x,y), ∀y∈Y" — i.e., the (soft) value of querying x is exactly zero precisely when observing x cannot change the posterior over θ at all (a stronger, sufficient condition subsuming "cannot change the decision"). This is a direct, explicit instance of the classical decision-analytic zero-value statement, specialized to classification via GP/robust classifiers.

**Classical origin: EVSI/preposterior analysis and Howard's Information Value Theory.**
- Raiffa & Schlaifer, *Applied Statistical Decision Theory*, Harvard Business School, 1961 (https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Applied+Statistical+Decision+Theory-p-9780471383499; PDF mirror https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1961-raiffa-appliedstatisticaldecisiontheory.pdf) is the standard citation for "preposterior analysis" / expected value of sample information (EVSI), the framework in which the value of an experiment is defined as the expected reduction in Bayes risk (expected posterior loss) it produces, evaluated *before* the data are seen.
- Howard, "Information Value Theory," IEEE Transactions on Systems Science and Cybernetics, vol. 2, no. 1, pp. 22–26, 1966 (https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Information-Value-Theory-Howard/a7b3c2a88ca459d50010a33db8c2f113f1323e0c) is the standard companion citation, formalizing the value of (perfect or imperfect) information as the expected improvement in the decision-maker's payoff.
- The specific proposition "the value of information can never be less than zero, and it is exactly zero whenever the information would not change the decision-maker's optimal action" is standard textbook decision theory (see, e.g., the Wikipedia summary of the non-negativity result, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_of_information — confirmed text: "The value of information can never be less than zero since the decision-maker can always ignore the additional information and make a decision as if such information is not available"). I was **not able to locate, in the sources fetched, a single sentence in Raiffa & Schlaifer or Howard 1966 phrased exactly as "value of information is zero iff the decision does not change"** (their treatments derive it as a corollary of the concavity/convexity structure of Bayes risk as a function of the posterior, via Blackwell-type arguments, rather than stating it as a standalone theorem in that exact wording) — this precise "zero iff no decision flip" phrasing is the form in which MOCU (Yoon/Dougherty/Qian 2013; Zhao et al. 2021 Lemma 2) states it explicitly and provably for their setting, and it is a standard immediate corollary of the classical EVSI/preposterior-analysis framework (Raiffa & Schlaifer 1961; Howard 1966) rather than a verbatim quotation from those two sources as best I could verify.

## C. Robust GP classification with label noise

**Kim & Ghahramani (2008).**
"Outlier Robust Gaussian Process Classification," in *Structural, Syntactic, and Statistical Pattern Recognition* (SSPR/SPR 2008), LNCS 5342, Springer — https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-89689-0_93; PDF confirmed at https://mlg.eng.cam.ac.uk/pub/pdf/KimGha08a.pdf. Confirmed abstract: "We present a form of GPC which is robust to labeling errors in the data set. This model allows label noise not only near the class boundaries, but also far from the class boundaries." **Exact likelihood used (their Eq. 10, confirmed verbatim):**
  p(y_i | f_i) = ε + (1 − 2ε)·H(y_i f_i),  H(x) = 1 if x>0, else 0
i.e. a mixture of a uniform-random-label component (probability 2ε total, split ε/ε between the two classes) and a hard-margin (Heaviside step / sign) classifier. The paper explicitly notes it considered — and rejected in favor of the step-function form — a smoother probit-type link: "Other possible forms for the likelihood are a sigmoid function ... and a step function with a labelling error ε + (1−2ε)H(y_i f_i). In this paper we use the step function..." (I could not extract their exact earlier equation for the sigmoid/probit variant from the fetch, but its existence and the choice made is confirmed verbatim.) **On recall for minority/rare-class points:** the fetched text contains **no explicit discussion of sensitivity/recall trade-offs for rare positive clusters** — the paper's evaluation is framed entirely in terms of overall misclassification/error rate on outlier-contaminated benchmarks, not per-class recall. This is a genuine gap in the primary source relative to what the prompt asks about, not something I can responsibly claim the paper states.

**Hernández-Lobato, Hernández-Lobato & Dupont (2011).**
"Robust Multi-Class Gaussian Process Classification," NeurIPS (NIPS) 2011 — https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2011/hash/7eabe3a1649ffa2b3ff8c02ebfd5659f-Abstract.html; PDF confirmed at https://mlg.eng.cam.ac.uk/pub/pdf/HerHerDup11.pdf. Confirmed abstract opening: "Multi-class Gaussian Process Classifiers (MGPCs) are often affected by overfitting problems when labeling errors occur far from the decision boundaries." **Exact likelihood (their Eq. 2, confirmed):**
  P(y | X, z, f) = ∏_i [ ∏_{k≠y_i} Θ(f_{y_i}(x_i) − f_k(x_i)) ]^{1−z_i} · (1/l)^{z_i},
a per-instance mixture between (a) the standard hard-max multi-class rule Θ (indicator that class y_i's latent function value exceeds every other class's) and (b) a uniform label over the l classes, gated by a binary latent "is this instance mislabeled" variable z_i — the natural multi-class generalization of Kim & Ghahramani's binary ε-mixture. **Again, no discussion of minority-class recall/sensitivity was found in the fetched content** — evaluation is again framed around aggregate error/overfitting reduction on contaminated benchmarks.

**Null-category noise model (the other major "robust to label structure" GPC likelihood family).**
Lawrence & Jordan, "Semi-supervised Learning via Gaussian Processes," NeurIPS (NIPS) 2004 — https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2004/hash/d3fad7d3634dbfb61018813546edbccb-Abstract.html. Confirmed: this is the paper that introduces the "null category noise model" (NCNM), inspired by ordered-categorical noise models, in which unlabeled points are treated as though they *could* have fallen in a third, "null," category near the decision boundary — this is a semi-supervised-margin device (pushing the boundary out of dense unlabeled regions) rather than a label-flip/outlier-robustness device per se, and should not be conflated with the ε-mixture robust likelihoods of Kim/Ghahramani and Hernández-Lobato et al., though it is frequently cited alongside them as part of the same "non-standard GPC likelihood" literature.

**Frénay & Verleysen (2014) survey.**
"Classification in the Presence of Label Noise: A Survey," IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, 25(5):845–869, 2014 — https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6685834; PDF mirror https://romisatriawahono.net/lecture/rm/survey/machine%20learning/Frenay%20-%20Classification%20in%20the%20Presence%20of%20Label%20Noise%20-%202014.pdf. Confirmed title/venue/year via IEEE Xplore and PubMed (24808033) records. This survey is the standard general reference distinguishing noise-at-random (NAR), noise-not-at-random (NNAR), and class-conditional label-noise models, and situates the ε-mixture likelihoods above within the broader taxonomy — the general form "true likelihood contaminated by a constant mislabeling-probability mixture" that both Kim/Ghahramani and Hernández-Lobato et al. use is the *class-conditional, symmetric* (NAR-like when ε is shared across classes) noise model in Frénay & Verleysen's taxonomy.

**On "does a symmetric robust likelihood hurt recall of genuine rare positives" — assessed, not found stated in the primary sources.**
None of the three primary papers fetched (Kim & Ghahramani 2008; Hernández-Lobato et al. 2011; Frénay & Verleysen 2014) contains an explicit statement, in the material retrieved, that a symmetric ε-contamination likelihood degrades recall of genuine small/rare positive clusters. This is, however, a direct and easily derivable consequence of the model structure itself: a *global, position-independent* ε shifts the entire likelihood surface toward the majority-class prior in any region where the GP's own confidence is high but the local labeled evidence is sparse, and it treats "a real small cluster of consistent positive labels surrounded by negatives" as observationally similar to "an isolated string of mislabeled negatives" — the model has no mechanism to distinguish a genuine rare cluster from ε-noise, because ε is *symmetric and spatially constant* by construction. This is an inference from the model form, not a documented finding in the surveyed papers, and should be labeled as such in any downstream writeup (i.e., it may be presented as your own analytical observation building on Kim/Ghahramani's and Hernández-Lobato et al.'s explicit likelihoods, but not attributed to them as their stated conclusion).

## D. Local / nonstationary boundary classifiers

**Mixture-of-experts GP models (regression, not classification, in their original form).**
- Tresp, "Mixtures of Gaussian Processes," NeurIPS (NIPS) 2000 (published 2001) — https://papers.nips.cc/paper/1900-mixtures-of-gaussian-processes; PDF http://papers.neurips.cc/paper/1900-mixtures-of-gaussian-processes.pdf. A finite mixture-of-experts model with GP experts, each specializing on a local region.
- Rasmussen & Ghahramani, "Infinite Mixtures of Gaussian Process Experts," NeurIPS (NIPS) 2001 — confirmed PDF at https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2001/file/9afefc52942cb83c7c1f14b2139b09ba-Paper.pdf. Confirmed abstract: "We present an extension to the Mixture of Experts model, where the individual experts are Gaussian Process regression models. Using an input-dependent adaptation of the Dirichlet Process, we implement a gating network for an infinite number of Experts." Confirmed: **each GP-expert may learn different lengthscales/noise variances**, giving genuinely local, non-shared amplitude/lengthscale behavior — but confirmed this paper addresses **regression only** (motorcycle-impact benchmark), not classification. Any claim of a classification variant of infinite-mixture GP experts would need a separate citation (e.g., subsequent Meeds & Osindero, "An Alternative Infinite Mixture of Gaussian Process Experts," NIPS 2005, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/2976248.2976359, which likewise remains primarily a regression-focused extension — not independently re-verified for a classification variant here).

**Nonstationary kernels.**
Paciorek & Schervish, "Nonstationary Covariance Functions for Gaussian Process Regression," NeurIPS (NIPS) 2003 (published 2004) — https://papers.neurips.cc/paper/2350; PDF (presentation slides, confirmed) https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~paciorek/presentations/paciorek-nips.pdf. Confirmed core construction: kernel-convolution nonstationary covariance R_NS(x_i,x_j) = c_ij ∫ k_i(u)k_j(u)du with spatially-varying local kernel matrices Σ(x), giving locally varying effective lengthscale/smoothness (confirmed example: "sample functions are more wiggly in the left part of the input space where the kernels are less broad"). Confirmed the paper explicitly states the framework "generalizes to non-Gaussian data" via a link function, "Y_i ~ D(g(f(x_i)))," with Bernoulli/binary classification as a named example — so this **does extend to nonstationary GP classification**, allowing a locally tight effective lengthscale (i.e., a sharply-varying boundary) in one region without imposing that sharpness (or its opposite, over-smoothing) globally. This is a genuine candidate for "a small cluster of positives with a locally different decision boundary," achieved through continuously-varying kernel geometry rather than hard partitioning.

**Treed GP classification — confirmed the most direct match for "small isolated positive cluster with no global amplitude cap."**
Broderick & Gramacy, "Classification and Categorical Inputs with Treed Gaussian Process Models," Journal of Classification 28(2):244–270, 2011 (arXiv:0904.4891, 2009) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00357-011-9083-y; https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.4891; full text https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/0904.4891. Confirmed abstract: "Recognizing the successes of treed Gaussian process (TGP) models as an interpretable and thrifty model for nonparametric regression, we seek to extend the model to classification." The tree partitioning recursively splits the input space into leaf regions, **each with its own independent local GP classifier** fit only to the data in that leaf, with no shared global amplitude/lengthscale constraint across leaves — this is precisely the mechanism that lets a small, spatially coherent cluster of one class inside an otherwise opposite-class region be isolated into its own leaf and fit with its own (possibly very different) local decision function, unconstrained by the classifier's behavior elsewhere. Of all the models reviewed, **this is the cleanest, most explicit match** for "a small cluster of positives inside a negative region, modeled without a global amplitude cap."

**Jump GP — confirmed regression-only, not classification (correction to the prompt's framing).**
Park, "Jump Gaussian Process Model for Estimating Piecewise Continuous Regression Functions," Journal of Machine Learning Research, 23(278):1–37, 2022 (not JASA, as might be assumed) — https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v23/21-1472.html. Confirmed: this is a **regression** method (piecewise-continuous function estimation via local data-partitioning at each test location); the fetched abstract and content give no indication of a classification variant.
